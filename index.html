Women's Lives in Developing Countries

Introduction

In many countries, women and girls are treated very differently to men and boys. While men are seen as the breadwinners and decision makers in the household, women are seen as the caregivers and custodians. The following video is episode one of our series: the patriarchy, a show that displays the difference in opportunities in these countries. Episode 1 is about the difference in everyday life between the two genders.

Full Interviews

This is the full interview of Dr. Harsheet Sethi: Samiya: Hello, I am Samiya Lockwood, and today I am here with my mother, Dr. Harsheet Sethi, a highly trained surgeon who has travelled around the world studying medicine and experiencing a lot of different healthcare systems. She has studied and worked in places such as the UK, rural India, metropolitan India and Australia, and has seen a lot of things in other countries as well. Today, I will be conducting an interview about women's health and education in developing countries, and how they link to various other aspects of this issue. I now welcome Dr. Harsheet Sethi. Dr. Harsheet Sethi: Thank you so much. Samiya: So, Dr. Sethi, what would you say are some things that prevent health equality from becoming a reality? Dr. Harsheet Sethi: That's an interesting question, thank you. Gender gaps are key factors that impact health outcomes. We do know that all across the world, women outlive men, but do they necessarily have longer, healthier lives? They do live with more chronic illness, and if they're asked if they're leading healthy lives, they wouldn't necessarily acknowledge themselves as leading healthy lives. So, it does come down to perceptions as well. Some of the barriers to gender equality are things that start with deeply seated social and cultural norms. These can be conscious and unconscious biases about gender and about perceptions of what is seen as important to an individual's family or their circumstances. For instance, in rural India, there is gender discrimination right from conception. When babies are born, boys are welcomed much more happily and with overtones of being more valuable to families, whereas girls are not. I think it's mostly rooted in this fear that women are going to be financially dependent on their fathers for the rest of their lives, and that families are going to have to spend money to get them married. They would potentially have poorer health outcomes as well. Whereas boys are seen as the strong males who are going to carry on the family name. They're going to study and work hard, earn a living, and contribute to the family's economic status. But coming back to opportunities for health and education, actually, they're quite interlinked because opportunities for a good education would lead to a bit more equality around health outcomes as well. For instance, there is very little awareness of what's needed. Going back to gender discrimination, people may be more willing to look after the health needs of boys. For example, baby health checks, vaccinations, making sure they're getting a nutritious diet with protein, and so on. In a lot of Indian families, the women, particularly the mothers in the house, will not prioritise self-care. They'll look after everyone in the family. So, if they have a small amount of protein to feed the family, which is, say, a bowl of lentils, they'll prioritise the men eating that. So, it starts even with things like a poor diet because the working men who are going and doing physical labour on the farms will be prioritised to get that protein-rich, nutritious meal. There's also internalised bias because often the men are not asking for that, but that's what the women think their role as a caregiver is: to really look after the breadwinners of the house. The other concern about health inequality is also around poor funding, particularly for research into healthcare. A lot of the research that is done is focused entirely on males, and so there isn't enough data available about gender inequalities and how we can fix the issues that are caused by that. But saying that, it's not always women who are disadvantaged. There are some societal beliefs and pressures about the way, for instance, a man or a woman would respond to emotional trauma or pain. Women are seen as—it's okay for them to be hysterical and vocal about their pain and talk about it. Perhaps talking about it is a good way to deal with it. Whereas men are always taught to be strong and solid and suffer, and maybe they internalise the trauma more. It can lead to more mental health problems, whether they're diagnosed or not. That leads to gender inequality as well, and maybe some of those mental health problems then go on to impact other things in society. It's also important to see that there aren't enough government rules or laws prioritising gender equality in healthcare. For instance, women's health, particularly around childbearing age, and when it comes to sanitary health education and providing them with the basics, such as sanitary napkins—it doesn't happen in rural India. They're still using dirty cloth during those times, and that can cause infections and UTIs. They're too embarrassed to talk about it. So even very treatable diseases are not getting treated as efficiently as they should. Samiya: Thank you for that. Now for our second question. In your experience working across the globe, what are some similarities and differences you've seen between countries? Dr. Harsheet Sethi: Thank you for that. I would say one similarity is that, to some degree, there are attempts to provide universal healthcare, at least for preventable diseases. For instance, in Australia, the government will provide this. With the WHO, or World Health Organization, coming in, they have also been able to roll out some projects across India and sub-Saharan Africa, for instance, for preventative health. So there are vaccination drives. The aim is to control or even eliminate diseases such as smallpox, measles or polio. The vaccines are provided and there are health drives to provide these vaccines. You know, they make free vaccinations available in the Western world, whereas there are camps and drives in underdeveloped countries. As we've seen during COVID, once these diseases spread and become a pandemic-level illness, it impacts absolutely everyone in the world. So preventative healthcare is very important. The other similarity would be in the sphere of education. Despite all the cultural barriers for girls to go to school—for instance, families don't want them to travel alone for several miles to get to their schools—the government is mandating schooling for children under 14, and child labour has been banned in India as well. They then try to make school more attractive to poorer families by providing school lunches, free textbooks and standard uniforms. There's always concern about misappropriation of funds, and I guess that's a similarity as well. It's just the scale to which it matters. The other similarity I'd say between developing countries and developed countries would be that, despite good universal healthcare, there still seems to be two tiers of healthcare and schooling available in both those countries. For instance, the private schools and hospitals in India, particularly in metropolitan India, would challenge and rival the best hospitals in the US or in Australia. The same applies to schools. They have the same IB education in India as they do in Australia, but that's only if you can afford it. So the difference is that the basic standard, for instance, in Australia for a public hospital or public school guarantees that you can get a good-quality education or healthcare without needing to pay for it, whereas that basic standard doesn't get met in India. There are free schools available, but the standard of teaching and the curriculum there, at Year 2, is not the same, and there is a disparity there. There's also less of a focus on achieving gender equality. Some ways to circumvent it in India would be through quotas for women in politics, education, schooling and employment, but it's not really uplifting women because this will take several years of change. Samiya: Thank you, next question. What impacts does a lack of good health and education have on the quality of life? Dr. Harsheet Sethi: Okay, so a lot of the impact can be both on mental health as well as physical health. General life expectancy in developing countries such as India is much shorter than in Australia or the UK. Part of that is because they're not educated about self-care and exercise. So there is very limited physical exercise. They cannot prioritise nutritious meals because nutritious meals cost more than starchy carbohydrates, which they tend to eat more of. An average meal would be bread and onion, which has no real nutrition, or just rice with rice water. They'll occasionally have lentils, and meat is a treat, so that's not really building them up for good health. That does lead to generally feeling lethargic and weak. They tend to be underweight and undernourished. They tend to have anaemia, which then leads to early fatigue. They'll often have undiagnosed chronic illnesses that don't get treated in time, so people can often die of a massive heart attack in their 40s, which would not be something that we would accept as normal in the developed world. The lack of education contributes to poor health because they're not aware of what they need to do. For instance, most poor people would not be visiting a dentist every six months or an optician every year. Most of them would not have a regular GP that they go and see. Now, some of that is because of their own prejudices and mistrust in modern medicine and reliance on herbal medicine and traditional medicine. But some of that is poor access to healthcare. And this really does impact their day-to-day life. It makes their life shorter. It also then makes them much more dependent in their older age, and there is no provision from the governments for aged care or old-age homes. So the dependency is then on their families. And then that leads to other crowding because families are living in smaller places, with parents and children and families all perhaps in the same one- or two-bedroom house in the cities. The burden of providing for a much larger family then falls on the single breadwinner, the male of the family, who then cannot afford to take a day off from work to look after their health. So it all gets perpetuated. Chronic illness is more common, their mental health suffers because of it, and they have shorter lifespans. Samiya: That is depressing. Dr. Harsheet Sethi: It is. I think it's also important to note that the lack of education creates some of that fear that women are not financially independent. When they're not financially independent, not only do they not look after themselves very much, they don't feel like contributing members of society, and their self-worth is questioned by themselves as well. Thankfully, this has changed, particularly in towns and cities, where women have recognised that they can get an education. This doesn't have to be a university education. It could be trade school. It could be training on the job, for instance, to be in a call centre or work in a mall. They could get trained for a particular trade, or they do now sometimes go to university to learn how to do typing, for instance. There are outsourced healthcare letters that get outsourced to India for typing. So women are getting job opportunities because they've recognised that if they get some education and they're physically active and healthy, they can be financially independent and then look after themselves better. Samiya: That's uplifting. Let's hope that does continue to change over time. How is education linked to good health outcomes for people? Dr. Harsheet Sethi: So it's extremely important to educate people about their health. If you go to school and finish a degree, it doesn't necessarily make you health literate. So health literacy is extremely important. That is done by outreach services in rural India. For instance, they have nurses reaching out, and they've got primary health centres. They've got a doctor where the tribal ladies could come and talk to. They have flyers and camps. They've got health camps going out to schools where they're able to do a general health check for kids who've never come to a GP or had their hearing or eyes tested. All of this is a part of their education to the families. That also helps to get over the deep-seated fear of modern medicine because once you teach them and show them that the things they fear are treatable ailments, they're more likely to use it. But the lack of that education increases the fear, the lack of engagement and poor health outcomes. Samiya: Oh, that's enlightening. And finally, before we finish, is there anything else you'd like to add? Dr. Harsheet Sethi: Yeah. I think it's important to remember that the gender imbalance is heavily tilted towards women not getting their share, but it's not always disadvantaging women. And that's important to keep in mind so we don't swing around too far the other way. We've got to remember it's about creating a balance. We don't want to be overworking them. We need to teach them to acknowledge their weaknesses and get their healthcare needs attended to in a timely fashion. We also want women to acknowledge that they do not have to take all the caregiving burden on their shoulders. They can teach their sons and daughters to be equal and prioritise education for young girls so they can be financially independent and make a better life for themselves and their families in the future. Samiya: Thank you for your time today, Dr. Sethi, and we hope that this will all be made better in the future.
